Building repairs

D65's schools are old, and they need a lot of work. We'll talk about the costs, and why numbers are hard to pin down.

1 · The buildings

Old buildings that need a lot of repairs.

D65 has 16 school buildings, averaging 76 years old. Nationally, the average public school's main building is about 49 years old.

The district says only 3 of the 16 are in "good" condition.

What kind of repairs do they need?

The big parts of a building that wear out over time: roofs, windows, and heating and cooling systems.

This year, the district did a study to assess how much work each school needs, what kind of work, and what year it should be done. More on that in a moment.

2 · The money

For years, D65 spent very little on building improvements.

From 2020-21 through 2024-25, D65 spent about $1.1 million a year on building improvements. Our calculation

The district's building study concluded it should be spending $20.6 million a year. That's a common rule of thumb: each year, set aside about 4% of what it would cost to rebuild your buildings.

D65 has been spending much less than neighbors. From 2014-15 through 2023-24, it spent about $330 a year per student. The typical neighbor spent about $2,500. Our calculation

Do districts really follow the 4% rule of thumb?

Typically, no. Across the country, districts spend about 2.4% a year, versus the 4% rule of thumb. Our calculation

But D65 has spent much less than that. Our calculation

3 · The gap

By putting off repairs, D65 now has a long, costly to-do list.

A district building study, done this year, lists the repairs and replacements each school needs, with a cost and a year for each.

That list calls for D65 to spend about $41 million a year for 10 years, across all the buildings. Our calculation

Wait. Didn't the study say we need to spend about $20 million a year?

That's just the rule of thumb.

The $41 million is what it would take to catch up, and keep up, over 10 years. That number also includes repairs for two schools that have closed and two more the district proposes closing.

So what's the plan?

The chart below shows what D65 plans to spend on building improvements in coming years. This year, $6.9 million, then working up to $8.3 million annually. District estimate

The district also plans to borrow about $9 million in health and life safety bonds for work in 2027 and 2028. District estimate

Note how far short the plan falls of both the rule of thumb and the study's catch-up number. Our calculation

Even at the plan's higher budget, most of the study's list wouldn't get done at this pace.

4 · The closures

What closing Dewey and Lincolnwood means for repairs.

The district says it will keep both buildings. They "will not be sold, leased, or repurposed in the short term."

Longer term, it says Dewey could serve as a "swing school," holding students while another school is rebuilt or renovated. It calls the Lincolnwood campus an "ideal site for a potential renovate or rebuild."

How much repair work would be avoided by closing them?

The district says closing the two schools would avoid $58 million in repairs over 20 years. That's about $3 million a year. District estimate Our calculation

Does that $3 million a year saved account for the cost of keeping the empty buildings?

The district didn't say. Its $58 million is close to the study's full repair list for the two buildings, which suggests it doesn't. Our calculation

So what does it cost to keep the empty buildings?

The district hasn't said.

Day-to-day costs, like heat, insurance and security, appear to be counted elsewhere. When the district lowered its yearly savings from closing the two schools, from $2.9 million to $2.3 million, part of the reason was that insurance and upkeep continue on buildings it keeps. After added busing costs, it puts the savings at about $2.1 million a year. District estimate

It hasn't said what bigger repairs an empty building would still need, such as a new roof.
