What's going on with District 65 schools?

It's a lot to follow. This guide can help.

More people should be able to understand what's happening in D65.

This guide explains the situation in a neutral, calm way, one idea at a time. It's meant to help as many people as possible understand what's going on.

We explain things in a conversational way, with helpful charts.

A moment from the story

Don't we pay really high property taxes?

We do. Evanston homeowners pay nearly twice the national rate and, in dollars, nearly three times the typical American's bill.

We give a lot of context. Some may be new to you.

  • D65 takes in more money per student than nearly nine in ten Illinois elementary districts, and nearly nine in ten U.S. elementary districts with 1,000 or more students.
  • About 1 in 5 D65 students now has a special education plan. Ten years ago, it was about 1 in 8.
  • Births to D65 families fell 31% over a decade.

We add historical perspective.

Has this happened before?

Yes, in the 1970s. But then, enrollment fell because there were fewer children. This time, it's falling much faster than the number of children.

The whole story in 2 minutes

Jump into the full story at any point, or tap a source to check it.

  1. D65 is trying to cut spending, which is disruptive and painful.It has already made cuts, and it's proposing more. Families are being asked to absorb a lot of change, and residents disagree about whether the changes are necessary, enough or fair.Read more
  2. D65 has more money than most districts.It receives more money per student than nearly 90% of elementary districts in Illinois, and of U.S. elementary districts with 1,000 or more students. Most of it comes from local property taxes, and most of it pays people to work there.Read more
  3. For the last few years, it has spent more than it takes in.After five years of surpluses, it has spent more than it took in every year since 2022-23 except 2024-25, which was close to break-even.Read more
  4. Its income can grow only a little each year.State law limits how much more property tax it can collect, usually 3% or less a year.Read more
  5. Its costs have grown faster.Pay and benefits usually rise 4% to 6% a year. After 2019, D65 also added staff, and special education spending rose about 75%.Read more
  6. For a while, extra money hid the gap.Surpluses from a 2017 tax increase, then federal pandemic aid. The savings were drawn down, and federal aid ended in 2024.Read more
  7. But D65 had little saved, and some decisions made it worse.It kept less in reserve than its neighbors. Budgets adopted as balanced in 2022 and 2023 understated costs. And a new school is paid for out of the everyday budget.Read more
  8. So the district has been cutting.About $26 million a year and more than 170 jobs since 2024, with about $20 million more proposed, mostly jobs.Read more
  9. Separately, there are far fewer students.About a quarter fewer than in 2016. That doesn't cost the district money, but it leaves the elementary schools about two-thirds full.Read more
  10. That's why closing schools is part of the plan.Two closed this year. Two more, Dewey and Lincolnwood, are proposed. The district estimates they would save $2.1 million a year after added busing, about $1 million each. Closures save a small part of the money, but they are what people argue about most.Read more
  11. Meanwhile, Foster was built to right a decades-old wrong.In 1967, to integrate its schools, the district stopped running a neighborhood school in the Fifth Ward, a mostly Black neighborhood, and bused its children elsewhere. Foster opened there this August.Read more
  12. Many don't trust D65's leadership, or its numbers.Leaders kept changing, a former superintendent pleaded guilty to federal crimes, and the district's own figures have been corrected.Read more

What's next? The board is expected to vote on the plan on Nov. 16. More decisions are coming on cuts, closures and new school boundaries. This guide will keep up.

About this guide

Who made it

My name is Andy Kerns. I moved to Evanston in 2024, and my daughter is in kindergarten at Lincoln Elementary.

I've been stunned by how much time, context and specialized knowledge it takes to keep up with our school district, and to hold an informed opinion about it.

I have experience with data storytelling, so I created this resource to explain what's happening with D65 in a calm, neutral, plainspoken way. It will remain free of opinions and proposals, and it's not affiliated with the district, the board or any parent group.

How it's built

  • Every fact traces to a public source. More than 900 sources, including district budgets, memos and presentations; board meeting videos and transcripts; state and federal data; local reporting; public-records requests; and analyses by residents and volunteer groups, labeled as theirs.
  • Every page is checked line by line against its sources before it goes up.
  • AI helped build this. I have experience using AI for research, data analysis, fact-checking and more. Both Claude and Codex were used.

Neutrality

This guide contains no opinions, proposals or endorsements. It is fully independent: not affiliated with or paid for by the district, the board, any union, parent group or campaign.

It strives to be accurate, clear and fair to everyone.
