The story

What's going on with District 65 schools?

Why D65 is short of money, why it's closing schools, and why so many people are upset.

1 · What's going on

District 65 is trying to cut spending, which is disruptive and painful.

District 65 runs the public schools for Evanston and part of Skokie, from preschool through eighth grade. That's nearly 6,000 students. ETHS is a separate district, with its own budget and leaders.

For the last few years, D65 has spent more than it takes in.

It has already made cuts, and it's proposing more: fewer staff, changes to services, and closing two additional schools.

Families are being asked to absorb a lot of change. And residents disagree about whether the changes are necessary, enough or fair.

2 · Money in

D65 has a lot of money. Why is it running out?

D65 takes in more money per student than nearly nine in ten Illinois elementary districts, and nearly nine in ten U.S. elementary districts with 1,000 or more students.

Illinois also estimates what each district needs to educate its students, counting how many are low-income, learning English or in special education.

By that yardstick, D65 has about 139% of what it needs. Only about one in ten Illinois elementary districts is better funded. Our calculation

That makes sense. Don't we pay really high property taxes?

We do. Evanston homeowners pay nearly twice the national rate and, in dollars, nearly three times the typical American's bill. Local property taxes pay for most of D65.

Still, a district receiving a lot of money can spend more than it takes in.

After five years of surpluses, D65 has spent more than it took in every year since 2022-23 except 2024-25, which was close to break-even.

Wow. What happened?

3 · Budget squeeze

Costs grew faster than the money coming in.

Neighboring districts face the same tax cap. Illinois caps how much more property tax a district can collect each year: inflation or 5%, whichever is lower.

In most years, that's 3% or less. Meanwhile, nothing caps how fast pay and benefits rise, which is usually 4% to 6% per year.

OK, what's unique to D65?

Several things piled up:

  • Through 2023-24, costs rose faster than most neighbors'. After 2019, D65 added staff, and special education costs rose about 75%.
  • Savings shrank, and federal aid ended. Surpluses from a 2017 tax increase were spent down and federal pandemic aid ended in 2024.
  • Budgets looked better than they were. A consultant found that budgets presented as balanced in 2022 and 2023 were not.
  • Financial controls were weak. A forensic review found loose handling of contracts, approvals and records, especially from 2021 to 2023. Controls were tightened starting in 2023-24.
  • A new school was built, paid for out of the everyday budget. Foster's yearly construction payment is about $3.2 million. The district expected bus savings to cover it. A later review found the records didn't support a rise in that estimate.

On top of all that, D65 had less saved to begin with than any of its neighbors.

Tell me more about increased spending.

4 · Money out

It's mostly people costs. Paying more to existing staff, and adding staff.

Most school spending pays for people, about three of every four dollars spent. Our calculation

D65 isn't alone in facing budget issues. In Illinois, the number of districts spending more than they took in nearly doubled in one recent year, and this past year, more than half of districts expected to run a deficit.

How much has D65's spending increased?

Between 2019-20 and 2023-24, it rose by about $46 million a year.

How much of that is inflation?

A lot of it. Prices rose 20% over those four years.

But D65's spending grew faster than prices, and faster than most of its neighbors'.

This chart shows where the added dollars went, in the years spending grew fastest.

So most of it went to salaries?

Counting benefits, yes: salaries and benefits added about $28.4 million, about 60% of the increase.

Salaries rose for two reasons. The same staff cost more each year. And D65 added staff after 2019, while it was losing students.

That's confusing. Fewer students, but more staff?

The biggest growth was in special education. Special education teachers grew by three-quarters.

All other teachers went down, by 20%. Our calculation

Why did special education grow so much?

More students have a plan for extra help.

When a student with a disability needs special help to learn, by law, they get a written plan for that help, called an IEP.

The most common reason is a learning disability, like dyslexia, that makes reading, writing or math hard. Many others are for children who need help with speech, or young children who are behind in talking, moving or getting along with others. Some are for ADHD or autism.

Once a child has a plan, federal law requires the district to provide the help it calls for.

Is this a broader trend?

Yes, but it's growing faster here.

For years, about 1 in 8 D65 students had a plan. In 2014-15, that was below most neighbors.

Now it's about 1 in 5, tied for the highest of 17 districts nearby.

So how much more are we spending on special education?

That growth shows up in two of D65's fastest-rising costs: busing and contracted help. Most of D65's busing costs are for special education, and most of its contracted help is special education staff.

An outside review and the district point to several causes:

  • More students placed in outside schools, at higher tuition.
  • Costlier special education busing, much of it through one taxi-and-van broker hired during a driver shortage.
  • Contract aides, who generally cost more than employees.
  • No in-district option for some students who need a separate classroom.

I've heard about busing costs. What's that all about?

Before the pandemic, D65 spent about what its neighbors did per student, or a little less. By 2023-24 it spent the most of the 17.

Special education drives the difference: it was 71% of D65's busing costs in 2024-25. D65's regular busing costs are in line with what neighbors spend per student.

D65's claimed busing costs fell 18% in 2024-25.

You mentioned there are fewer students in D65. What's the story there?

5 · Declining enrollment

D65 has lost a quarter of its students since 2016. No neighbor has lost more than 13%.

This fall, by the district's own count, D65 had 5,406 students in kindergarten through 8th grade, 4% fewer than a year before. It projects about 4,900 by 2031-32. District estimate

Why? Where did all those kids go?

Two things happened at once.

First, fewer babies were born here. Births to D65 families fell 31% over a decade. And the number of young children living here fell faster than in any neighboring district.

Second, fewer of the children who live here go to D65. From 2020 to 2024, the number of school-age children living in the district fell about 5%. D65's enrollment fell about 20%, from 2019-20 to 2024-25. Our calculation

Has this happened before?

Yes, in the 1970s. But then, enrollment fell because there were fewer children. This time, it's falling much faster than the number of children.

We found no count of where those children go to school. Some go to private schools, and a few are homeschooled.

The losses sped up in fall 2020, when D65 stayed fully remote longer than several neighbors. Kindergarten fell 17% that fall, twice the state's drop. No one knows how many families left because of it. The biggest one-year drop came the next fall, in 2021.

Losing students doesn't reduce D65's income. Its costs don't go down unless it cuts them.

D65's elementary schools are now about two-thirds full.

Every one of those buildings still needs a principal, office staff, custodians, heat and repairs, full or not.

How full is a school supposed to be?

Illinois has no rule. D65's own 2022 building plan aimed for 85%, and some other districts aim for 80% to 100%.

Is that why they want to close schools?

6 · School closures

Closures save a small share of the money, but draw the most debate.

The district gives three reasons for closing schools: empty space, the teaching jobs closures would let it cut, and the cost of keeping up older buildings.

Since 2024, the district has cut about $26 million a year and more than 170 jobs. It now plans about $19.5 million more, mostly jobs. District estimate

How much does closing a school save?

About $2.1 million a year for the two, after added busing. That's real money, but about a tenth of the proposed cuts. The district says closing them would also let it cut about $1 million a year in teaching jobs. District estimate

Why these two schools?

The district says the middle-school areas they're in, Haven and Nichols, have each lost about a third of their students in ten years. Nearby schools have room for the rest.

Even after the closures, the remaining schools would be about three-quarters full, short of the district's 85% goal. District estimate

What do people who oppose the closures say?

Some question the savings. The district's estimate fell from $2.9 million to about $2.1 million in a month, and it plans to keep both buildings.

Others point to the disruption for students, including former Kingsley families who would switch schools again.

Wait, didn't the district just open a new school?

It did. Foster opened in the Fifth Ward this August. It wasn't built for space: D65's schools already had empty seats when the board approved it in 2022.

To understand why it was built, you need the history behind it.

7 · Foster School

Foster was built to right a decades-old wrong.

In 1967, to integrate its schools, the district stopped running a neighborhood school in the Fifth Ward, a mostly Black neighborhood. Instead, for decades, the ward's children were bused across town.

How is the district paying for Foster?

Out of its everyday budget: about $3.2 million a year, through 2042.

The board approved it expecting bus savings to cover that payment. The estimate peaked the night of the vote, then fell.

The review drew no conclusion about why.

Two things are true: Foster gave the Fifth Ward its neighborhood school back, and it adds to the current squeeze.

Some families facing closures are frustrated the district gave one neighborhood its school back, then asked them to give up their own.

Are there other reasons people are upset with the district?

8 · Leadership and trust

Many don't trust D65 leadership, or its numbers.

Trust has worn down for two reasons.

First, the people in charge keep changing. D65 has had five different superintendents or interim teams since 2019.

One of them, Devon Horton, pleaded guilty in October to federal wire fraud, embezzlement and tax evasion charges, and admitted taking kickbacks from district vendors.

The forfeiture amounts in his plea agreement total about $314,000. That is small next to deficits of millions of dollars a year. But it hurt the district as an institution. Our calculation

Second, the numbers keep changing. Some changes were mistakes. Others were routine updates, or the same thing counted different ways. That makes it hard to know which number to trust.

9 · What's next?

What's next?

The board is expected to vote on the plan on Nov. 16. More decisions are coming on cuts, closures and new school boundaries.

This guide will keep up. When something big happens in the district, we'll explain what it means, in plain terms, with the evidence behind it.
